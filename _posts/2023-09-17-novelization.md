# Novelization and Its Discontents

As a lover of mass-market paperbacks, and especially paperback originals, one thing that particularly fascinates me is era, from the '50s to the '80s, during which paperbacks were written primarily to be sold. During this time writers like [Robert Silverberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Silverberg_bibliography), ["Big Bob" Tralins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Tralins), and [W.E.D. Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._E._D._Ross) wrote, over their careers, millions of words of prose *on command* from publishers. We think of the modern hardcover and trade-paperback industry as a numbers game, where the majority of books lose money on the way to print, but whose losses are made up on the profits from a select few runaway bestsellers. In the golden age of mass-market paperbacks, these cheap, disposable books were (with the right cover) guaranteed to sell; you just needed words to put inside them.

These words were almost never provided by writers of reputation; they were the domain of hacks, of pot-boiler technicians who wrote many books a year (Robert Silverberg wrote a novel a week when churning out formulaic "sex novels" for Greenleaf Press in the 1950s). These books could not be burdened by an artist's concern for originality, profundity, or stylistic virtuosity, but they were often (though certainly not always) undertaken with a craftsman's simple pride in their work. I admire these craftsmen who made a living, if not a name, with their writing, and I'd like to help bring to light some of those who were not also famous as artists (as Silverberg undoubtedly is).

One of the most well-known, and most derided, category of these books-to-order is the novelization: an upcoming film (less often, a television series or even a videogame) is adapted into a novel and published as part of a broader marketing campaign. Novelizations were almost always written by craftsmen rather than artists; only a very few (say, [Alan Dean Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Dean_Foster)) would ever become recognizable names, even to fans. These books were commissioned, written, printed, sold, and forgotten in the space of a year or less, and garnered next to no critical attention. They are book-length advertisements for other products; now that the products are no longer for sale, they sit gathering dust on the clearance shelves of second-hand bookshops.

In this series, I am going to reviews forgotten novelizations of famous films, paying special attention to the curious commercial forces that brought the book into being, the specific artistic voice the novelizer brings to the project and how they mesh (or don't mesh) with the voice of the original creator(s), and above all to the craft that goes into the final product.

## A Note on Criticism

I have no academic training in literary criticism (though my biases make me consider this as an advantage rather than a liability). I value art primarily as an act of communication, and I evaluate an artwork on that basis. I consider equally both *what* is being communicated and *how well* it is done. Sometimes, as is often the case with CS Lewis, the communication is so skilled that I read it even when I dislike what is being communicated. Sometimes, \[no example\], I like the *what* so much I can overlook deficiencies in the *how*. But certainly the ideal book says something worth saying, and says it well.

Likewise, as with any act of communication, it is natural to consider the person you are communicating with when trying to interpret what they have to say; thus I care more than is fashionable about an author's personal life and the opinions they themselves have expressed about their work. [Noël Carroll's excellent short book](https://www.routledge.com/On-Criticism/Carroll/p/book/9780415396219) *[On Criticism](https://www.routledge.com/On-Criticism/Carroll/p/book/9780415396219)* expresses fairly well my own attitude towards criticism.

My favorite critic *of* any medium *in* any medium is Roger Ebert; if I can bring a fraction of his wit, insight, compassion, discernment, and open mind to my work, I'll have succeeded beyond any natural right.

## A Note on Novelizations

By *novelization*, I mean only books that are direct adaptations of the source material they are intended to promote; books that were books first, and are re-released as [Will Smith editions](https://www.barnesandnoble.com/w/i-am-legend-richard-matheson/1007650491), do not count; neither do works derived from characters or settings in existing works but with new, "original" stories, so-called "tie-in" books like the Star Wars Expanded Universe. These books are also worth study, but do not fall under the arbitrary remit of this series as delineated by the pun in its title.
