As always, Carl Sagan said it better than anyone. Watch for about 60 seconds.

Here's the transcription of what he says:

> What an astonishing thing a book is. It's a flat object made from a tree with flexible parts on which are imprinted lots of funny dark squiggles. But one glance at it and you're inside the mind of another person, maybe somebody dead for thousands of years. Across the millennia, an author is speaking clearly and silently inside your head, directly to you. Writing is perhaps the greatest of human inventions, binding together people who never knew each other, citizens of distant epochs. Books break the shackles of time. A book is proof that humans are capable of working magic, and this room is full of magic.

He's right, of course. It is miraculous that we are able to write down our thoughts and share them with other people we've never met, to preserve them for centuries or millenia, to find the thoughts of other people so preserved.

But are books the only way to do that?

No. They aren't, and they haven't been for a long time. Probably they've never been.

All societies start out without writing, and they had traditions of storytelling and oral history for thousands or tens of thousands of years before any of it got written down. They're not perfectly reliable, of course, but they're better than you might expect, and obviously the written word is not perfectly reliable either.

Of course, most orally transmitted information is anonymous, and likely highly collaborative, so that although they are good way of preserving culture and knowledge, they are not good way to, as Carl says, hear an author "speaking clearly and silently, inside your head, directly to you." Even orally-transmitted stories associated with a specific author, like the Homeric epics, probably emerged collaboratively, and "Homer" may well have been a mythic figure who stands in for each bard in a long line, forgotten in their turn.

Books were the first method of getting down precisely [(more or less)](https://www.reddit.com/r/tolkienfans/comments/z2pruu/a_concise_list_of_lord_of_the_rings_textual/) what a specific person had thought or said or written, and sharing and preserving that record with other people, remote in time or place. From the earliest clay tablets and papyrus scrolls to Bluesky posts less than a second old, the technology of writing has not changed in its fundamentals, although its uses and usefulness have expanded considerably.

But we have new methods of recording, too: audio and video. The century and a half, roughly, that has elapsed since these methods were invented has seen a [dramatic shift in how most people](blog/2026/why-mass-markets/) experience the communion Carl describes. Hearing a voice from the past is just as likely, now, to refer to [looking at black-and-white photographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migrant_Mother) or [dusting off a wax cylinder recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zXwKrAkeSjw) as it is to reading a book written by a person now dead.

These methods are, in fact, wonderful and powerful. I come not to shit on mass media, but to praise it. Film and television and videogames are great forms of art, and if the Ancient Greeks were around today (the Modern Greeks must concede that they are not themselves ancient) they'd add a couple of muses for them. I do not think of film as a shallow medium incapable of communicating great ideas or profound emotions. So are books just obsolete now?

Well, first of all, obviously not. Books still keep coming out. They come out at [a prodigious rate](https://www.thewritermoibrahim.com/2016/09/over-50000-fiction-books-published-per.html). It seems to me that the vast majority of English-language books ever to exist have been published in the last 75 years, competing very well with movie and TV and radio and even videogames the whole time. It may be much more common to have seen a blockbuster movie than to have read a New York Times bestseller, but many people happily do both.

And yet, of all those tens of thousands of books released each year, only a handful are written by people who can afford not to do anything for their income but write. In the mid-20th century, being a writer was such a feasible career that Michael Crichton considered it a more attractive prospect than taking up a medical practice, and he wrote pulpy crime fiction to _pay for medical school_. This kind of money is no longer available to the "jobbing" writer. Published authors in the 21st century write because they _want_ to, and any money they make from it is beside the point.

But _why_? Why do books hold on? Writing can only communicate using language, and not even all of language at that: we can, at best, approximation intonation, emphasis, expression, gesture. Video can replicate nearly everything from two of our most important senses. Movies, as a product of the commercial industry that produces them, generally have to abridged and simplify books they adapt, but this is not an inherent property of the moving image. You can take an entire unabridged audiobook and illustrate it sentence-by-sentence in MSPaint and you have made a movie that replicates every discrete piece of information from the book. It's probably not an easy experience, but it's there.

I am not convinced by most arguments about what books or movies "can" or "can't" do, or that certain books are "unfilmable" or "peculiarly unsuitable to transformation into visual dramatic form." But I cannot deny that the experiences of reading a book and watching a movie are completely unalike, and completely incommensurable. Even very faithful adaptations, like _Rosemary's Baby_ or _The Queen's Gambit_ cannot literally replicate the original experience (and it would be absurd to try).

So what _is_ the difference? I think Carl Sagan's words captured it in a way I didn't even expect when I began this essay: "an author is speaking clearly and silently inside your head, directly to you." A movie, for instance, is such a collaborative enterprise on a grand scale that it is hard to relate to its content intimately. You know dozens of people, at a minimum, had their hand in its creation, and when you go see one in a theater you cannot escape the fact that other people are there, that this experience is for all of you. I think that experience is wonderful and can be very moving, and I have spent a not inconsiderable amount of my life in movie theaters and trying to convince other people to go to them.

A book is the _opposite_ experience. Despite the knowing authors have editors and agents and publishers, despite their acknowledgments and dedications, one cannot escape the feeling, when reading a book, that you are getting direct access to the author's thoughts - more direct, even, than talking to them, because there you cannot escape the fact that the author is another person who exists in time and space. Their words and their very identity come to life _in your mind_. While you are reading a book, the author is closer to you than any other person in your life can hope to be.

And yet this _is_ what makes book special. Still absolutely uniquely among all the art forms, books are cheap to produce. You don't need the backing of a major studio to hire a cast and crew of hundreds, you don't need a team of highly-paid ([but overworked!!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gMKBzwSBeS8)) artists and programmers, you don't need a recording booth and an audio engineer or even a guitar. Paper, ink, typewriter ribbons used to be meaningful expenses for the professional writer, but today those things are cheap beyond [Toklien's wildest dreams](https://kitdunsmore.com/2019/01/30/tolkien-perfectionism-paper/) or completely unnecessary. In a clinch, you don't even have to [be able to hold a pen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diving_Bell_and_the_Butterfly). You don't need anyone's permission. All you need to do to write is to want to.

Of course, that leads to the publication, or at least the dissemination, of all manner of unreadable dross. But it also leads to the creation of things that you would never see in an expensive, collaboritve medium like film. If you want to make a movie set on Mars, you need [about a hundred million dollars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Martian_(film)). To write [a book about the same thing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Martian_(Weir_novel)#Publishing_history), you only need your evenings free.
