# V for Vendetta

Halloween, in its current candy-and-costumes incarnation, is an American holiday, but it is spreading to many other cultures, as our mass media shows teenagers the world over what they're missing. Mexico is keeping a good hold on their Day of the Dead, but it is not hard to find old fogy Britons complaining about American Halloween replacing good old-fashioned English Bonfire Night.

Bonfire Night is, like Halloween, mostly an opportunity for teenagers to roam the streets after dark and raise hell, but the eponymous bonfires stem from a tradition of burning effigies of Guy Fawkes, who, in 1605, conspired with a number of English Catholics to blow up the houses of Parliament and install a Catholic monarch on the throne. Ever since, figures of straw or wood would be built and topped with papier-mâché masks with hand-drawn faces on then, and then immolated. It is from the ubiquity of these dummies that the word Guy went from being [a proper to a common noun](https://www.etymonline.com/word/guy), and it was from these masks that David Lloyd, when he and Alan Moore were brainstorming a political superhero comic in 1982, took inspiration in designing the character V[^1].

V dresses in all black, except for his haunting white mask, and he throws knives at fascists. He reads and quotes old books, watches old movies, is articulate, charismatic, and theatrical, and must perpetually conceal his hideously disfigured face (He just like me fr). This characterization of V is our most consistent through-line between all three - yes, *three* - incarnations of *V for Vendetta* we'll be looking at today: the 1982 comic, the 2006 film, and the 2006 novelization of the film.

Spoilers for all three will follow.

## What You Need is a Story

When discussing Edward Norton's 2019 film adaptation of his novel *Motherless Brooklyn,* Jonathan Lethem said, "The best thing is if the book is a dream the movie once had." To a reader of Alan Moore and David Lloyd's *V for Vendetta* who*,* like me, saw the movie first, this seems like an apt description. Some of the same events happen, to some of the same people (or at least people with the same or similar names), but not in the same order or for the same reasons. The story feels rushed in some places and languorous in others; some things are set up and never paid off, and some are paid off without ever having been set up; some scenes happen exactly as they do in the movie, and others seem not to have any reason for happening *except* that they happen in the movie. Except, of course, that it came first by 20 years.

I wish I could spend real time on the interplay between the comic and the film, but as I am not Jenny Nicholson or H. Bomberguy making a four-hour video essay, I will summarize the differences in broad strokes. The film sands down the rough edges of the source material (what fans of the original might characterize as "flattening" it): whereas V blows up Parliament right at the beginning of the comic, and blows up the Old Bailey and eventually Downing Street somewhat haphazardly and without any sense of progression, V in the film opens by blowing up the old Bailey and then promising to blow up Parliament in one year, giving the movie a ticking clock the comic lacks. Comic Evey is brought back to the Shadow Gallery immediately after the Parliament demolition and dropped off by V in the middle of London without ceremony or explanation when he thinks she's "ready"; film Evey is on her own until V's raid on Jordan Tower, after which he he brings her back to the Shadow Gallery to protect from the consequences of her decision to help him. When Evey leaves then, it is because of her own decision that she wants to escape and betray V. Gordon Detriech in the comics is just some guy, vaguely related to the regime but broadly sympathetic, that Evey happens to be living with; he is [fridged](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/StuffedIntoTheFridge) in the same issue we meet him, just to further traumatize Evey. In the movie, Gordon Detriech is Evey's old boss, an extremely sympathetic and somewhat radical figure in his own right, memorably played by Stephen Fry and his death, though also traumatizing to Evey, is important to the viewers *and* moves the plot forward as well.

Some odd, and perhaps oddly feminine, character traits are elided; Lewis Prothero no longer collects dolls; the comic's Adam Susan becomes the film's Adam Sutler (Susan + Hitler, I only *just* realized). Any sense of sympathy or even interiority we have with Susan is erased, and Sutler becomes any other ruthless, conservative demagogue. The comic's Peter Creedy is a featureless mid-season replacement for the previous featureless leader of the Finger, who V kills incidentally in the middle of another operation. The time we spent with his mourning widow trying to make her way in the patriarchal world of the Party is spent developing Creedy. Finch is fairly similar, except that he spends his time in Larkhill tripping on LSD, and never fully comes around to V's point of view. He is the final character we see, leaving V's anarchic London forever like [those who walk away from Omelas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ones_Who_Walk_Away_from_Omelas). For some reason, his final act is leaving a female party member to be sexually assaulted by a group of toughs.

## The Novelization

The movie was controversial for two main reasons: first, the watering down of V's radical politics, and secondly, ignorance of or indifference to British culture. The novelization was written by Steve Moore, a British comics writer who is *not* related to Alan Moore, but was a very close friend of his. It is very clear throughout that his goal as the novelizer was to rectify both issues while sticking as close as possible to the story in the screenplay. He is not subtle about this (or about anything, but more about that later), to the extent that some of his narration comes off as downright passive-aggressive.

In the film, V and, later, Gordon Detriech, fry eggs in a hole in a piece of toast, which Detreich calls "[eggy in a basket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_in_the_basket)." You will find plenty of angry Brits complain that this made-up name is insultingly tweet, and Steve Moore is on their side:

> In the Shadow Gallery's kitchen, V was smiling to himself beneath the mask, preparing what the Americans had called eggs in a basket, though, in the part of England where'd he'd come from, they were always called an Egyptian fish-eye sandwich. Quite why he never knew, but he treasured the picturesque name as another fine survival from the past. *(Chapter Six)*.

Why should V have knowledge of what Americans call this breakfast dish? Sorry, what *the* Americans call it? The phrase can only refer to the Americans who wrote the screenplay.

In the comic and in the film, one of V's signatures is leaving a rose by the corpses of the people he's killing in revenge. They are [Violet Carson roses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_%27Violet_Carson%27) in the comic, but they are changed to "Scarlet Carsons" in the movie, presumably because roses aren't purple, they're red. But Violet is the not the color of the rose, it's a [name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violet_Carson), and Steve Moore puts it right again in his book, as Dr. Delia Surridge explains to Inspector Finch:

> She started to tell him how it was a hybrid, dating back to 1963 and named after a popular actress who used to be in the TV soap opera *Coronation Street*. Was there anything there? Finch wondered, vaguely remembering the show from when he was a kid. What was the character's name? Ena Sharples, that was it. A working-class busybody whom he'd always, frankly, regarded as a bit of an old cow; but she'd been in the show for nearly twenty years and made herself a household name. Probably nothing, then... unless his man was somehow obsessed with popular culture from before the Reclamation. But, no, it was probably just the letter *V... (Chapter 12)*

There is no reason to stop this investigation scene dead it in its tracks *except* as a middle finger to the Wachowskis' apparent ignorance of *Coronation Street*.

Alan Moore never saw the final film, but he read the screenplay, and complained that the script had plot holes. He didn't go into detail, but Steve Moore makes a point in several scenes to have character think about how something could have happened, and then devise some explanation for how it makes sense after all.

Steve Moore's best moment comes when he is able to both identify a plot hole *and* reintroduce Alan Moore's radicalism. Inspector Finch realizes that the Larkhill facility where V was kept must have been in operation *before* Suttler's fascist coup, something that is still clearly true in the movie. It is never explained, there, why the ostensibly non-fascists UK government before Suttler's time would have operated such an awful place.

> *Could* they have gotten away with something so flagrant? And if they could, didn't that imply collaboration of the previous government...imply that, actually, Norsefire's fascism wasn't actually much worse than that of any other political party, Conservative or Labour, only more overt? Or was there an even broader conspiracy, where Norsefire was only the public face of a plot that had begun with the previous government, the one Norsefire had apparently overthrown, to abolish the old order and replace it with the new? But that would imply that the whole political system, whether Conservative, Labour, or Liberal, had become unutterably corrupt; had probably been so far years, and that even prewar elections were as farcical as the ones they had today. That, in the words of an old catchphrase of his youth, "whoever you voted for, the government still got in." And was the anarchism espoused by V, then, the only true political answer? *(Chapter 21)*

V hasn't, in this book, actually espoused anarchism. In fact, he repeats the line he gives in the film, "People shouldn't be afraid of their governments. Governments should be afraid of their people." This line badly distorts comic-V's (and Alan Moore's) anarchism: governments *shouldn't exist*. Steve does what he can to retain the flavor of that visionary V, but he feels too beholden to the screenplay to do this any more than symbolically, around the margins of the story and without meaningfully altering any lines of dialogue.

Some touches from the comic are snuck unobtrusively back in: the ornately decorated tube train in the finale; Ruth's explicit betrayal of Valerie (Evey, hearing the story, even infers Ruth's suicide!); Evey's being naked when she stands in the rain; V hacking into the Norsefire computer systems. The internet tells me Steve took this job with Alan's blessing, as he needed the cash; I have no doubt he felt obligated to his friend to reintroduce as much as he could of that friend's story. On that score, he did not fail.

## The Discontent

*V for Vendetta* was Steve Moore's first work of prose, and it shows. He was a comics writer; it was Steve who showed Alan how to write for the sequential arts. I hope it was not Steve who taught Alan how to write novels. Perhaps he needed to fill space to turn a 120-page screenplay into a 368-page book without adding any scenes, but he spells everything out for us: he tolerates no ambiguous language, no unexplained metaphors, no *doubles entedres* that are not made explicit. The majority of my dozens of notes on this book are examples of this kind of literal-mindedness, and it would lengthen an already-too-long review to cite all of them. Here are among the worst:

> "Spare the road, spoil the child," Willy snickered evilly, leering suggestively to his mates and starting to unzip his pants fly with exagerated, theatrical emphasis. Only one rod in mind there, then... *(Chapter Two)*

> "If the government ever searched my house, you would be the least of my problems." She started in blank inccomprehension. What could be worse than what she'd done in the last few days? And thinking only of herself, she completely missed that someone had said something similar to her only a few days \[*and only five chapters! - Ed*\] previously. *(Chapter Eleven)*

> Then he reached down in the lower drawer of his desk and pulled out a small electronic device and flicked it on before he said a single word.
>
> Dominic reacted in obvious surprise as he recognized the jammer, specifically designed, he knew, to knock out the Ear's bugs and listening devices. No one was supposed to have one of them. That was the sort of thing they arrested people for. *(Chapter Fourteen)*.

This example was especially egregious to me, as I routinely point out the signal jammer device as one of the movie's most elegant details, and emblematic of its approach to exposition: a piece of worldbuilding revealed entirely in shot composition and body language. No character ever stops to explain what it is for, because the film trusts us to understand. Steve Moore's book does not. Of course, I was used to his overdetermined prose by this point, and I gloated for a moment to my partner, because I had told her I expected him to botch exactly this thing.

> She'd give him a Viking funeral, like those old chieftains had had when they were set adrift in a blazing boat on a cold northern sea, sailing off through the fire and ice to arrive at last in Valhalla. A notion he would have enjoyed, she thought, with his passion for the letter *V*. *(Chapter Twenty-Four)*

His fidelity to the screenplay extends even to the film's final moments. What Evey gives *as narration* in the last scene is rendered here as diagetic dialogue, spoken *to nobody:*

> "No one will every forget this night," she told him, "or what it means to this country." And then, turning away once more for a last, private thought, "But I will never forget the man, and what he meant to me." *(Chapter Twenty-Four)*

It may come as no surprise to readers of this review that I love the movie and feel pretty unenthusiastic about the graphic novel, but while this adaptation tries to be the best of both worlds, combining the source's radicalism and authenticity with the film's tighter narrative, it is so deeply undermined by Steve's prose that I can recommend only as fodder for a drinking game. Take a shot every time something implied in one sentence is stated explicitly in the following sentence. That will make for a November the Fifth that will definitely be forgot.

[^1]: I had always assumed the mask V wore was *the* canonical Fawkes mask *from* Bonfire Night celebrations, but it turns out there was no such canonical mask. The design is Lloyds's own, and they were never mass-produced until the release of the film.
