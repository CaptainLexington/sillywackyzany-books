You've heard this story before. FW Murnau made his adaptation of the 1897 novel *Dracula* without permission from Bram Stoker's estate, and his widow Florence sued him after *Nosferatu's* release in 1922. She eventually won the lawsuit, and all copies of the film were ordered to be destroyed. One or a few or [several prints survived](https://www.npr.org/2022/03/15/1086605684/try-as-she-might-bram-stokers-widow-couldnt-kill-nosferatu), and the movie remains today as a horror classic, [endlessly re-released with new scores](https://www.nosferatu-radiohead.com). An important piece of film culture was saved because the physical medium on which it was preserved (in this case, celluloid) survived.

Now another story you've heard before: Danny Boyle's famous, beloved, groundbreaking 2002 independent zombie film *28 Days Later* is not streaming anywhere today, and the Blu-ray is out of print. A used copy will run you about \$40 on eBay at time of writing, and that is the only way to watch it.

But this tweet has it exactly backwards: the people in *Fahrenheit 451* committed books to memory because the physical media on which those books were originally distributed *could be destroyed*. At the risk of [overextending the computer-brain metaphor](https://aeon.co/essays/your-brain-probably-is-a-computer-whatever-that-means), memorizing a book is converting it *into* digital information. This is *how* you prevent it from being burned.

The Library of Congress estimates that [75% of American silent films are lost.](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-film-preservation-board/documents/pub158.final_version_sept_2013.pdf) There was no concerted effort to ban these films, to suppress them or destroy them. They simply went away. They were thrown out as expendable, or [they burst into flames](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hb7a4ylaJxI), or [they turned into vinegar](https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/2020/06/19/film-preservation-101-why-does-this-film-smell-like-vinegar/). Or they [languished in a closet for 70 years,](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2008/jul/03/news.culture3) and perhaps some languish there still.

Nowadays, of course, we treat our movies better: we keep archival quality copies in climate-controlled vaults, where they undergo regular inspection and maintenance to ensure they remain in pristine condition. But this system is not perfect: [in 2008, archival copies of tens of thousands of movies, TV shows, and album recording sessions were lost in a construction fire at the Universal Studios backlot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Universal_Studios_fire?). There is dispute over how much that was lost was truly irreplaceable, but so far it is believed that everything that burned had *some* kind of existing copy somewhere else.

Okay, but what about the TV shows and movies being pulled from streaming services left and right, now completely inaccessible, you ask? Well, in most cases, those things did not have physical media releases in the first place.

What about books? Electronic books are often wrapped in several layers of licensing, so that licenses can be [unilaterally rescinded without notice](https://ncac.org/news/blog/high-school-student-sues-amazon-for-deleting-1984-from-kindle), or books released in one market may be [unavailable in another](https://www.mobileread.com/forums/showthread.php?t=117828) (a problem [familiar to users of streaming services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_literary_work) as well). But here, again, physical media is [no guarantee of safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_literary_work). Books are among the most delicate and difficult-to-preserve of human artifacts. Books printed by the tens of thousands as little as fifty years ago now are difficult to find in readable condition (thanks everyone for buying them from me when I do). Many influential and well-respected books are out-of-print, and the small presses that pull them from obscurity to run limited edition reprints are usually labors of love that will never turn a serious profit.

I am not saying physical media is *bad*. I own hundreds of paper books and have no plans to get rid of them. Many people enjoy the rituals of getting a DVD off the shelf or putting a vinyl record onto a turntable. That's completely justifiable. But owning physical media is [not going to preserve our culture](https://gizmodo.com/how-long-will-your-blu-ray-collection-last-1851379157) for future generations. To do this, you will have to do what FW Murnau did when he made *Nosferatu*, and what the people who preserved the prints of that film did when they copied them, or when they failed to return them to the distributor, or when they refused to relinquish them to the court.

You're going to have to break the law.

I may not be able to stream *28 Days Later* on Netflix, or go down to Best Buy and pick the Blu-ray out of the discount bin, but I can get a copy on my hard drive in about 20 minutes. It will never be subject to licensing disagreements, because I have violated the terms of the license already. My access cannot be revoked by a third-party because their efforts to exert their control over that access have been thwarted in advance. Film buffs can shuffle the finite number of remaining Blu-rays between themselves indefinitely, as one by one each copy degrades or cracks or is lost, and the number of people hosting publicly-available copies will only grow over time. A copy of *28 Days Later* on your shelf preserves it only for yourself and whoever you can convince to come over to watch it; a copy on your hard drive preserves it for anyone with an Internet connection.

This is no doubt not what Danny Boyle wants, or even Guillermo del Toro, but it is the solution necessitated by the decisions of the rights-holding corporation. If media companies refuse to care about the stewardship of the culture they have financed (and they certainly have), it is no surprise that the people who love that culture will step in by whatever means necessary.

Physical media is a fine hobby, but the real vanguard of the fight for media preservation is piracy. Piracy created, and saved, *Nosferatu*. Who knows what it will save next?
