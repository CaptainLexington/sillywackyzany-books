The tech industry's fascination with generative AI is short-sighted. Not because the technology is not yet profitable; they may be able to make it profitable. Nor because the technology is environmentally unsustainable; they may be able to make it sustainable. It would be naive to expect any piece of technology, after surveying the last 6,000 years of human history, to remain forever in its most primitive and inefficient state. Even if AI becomes cheap and eco-friendly and dare I say it even good at its job, it will become a huge problem for technology companies, because most of them derive their value from the value of the Internet. And AI is going to destroy the value of the Internet. The better AI becomes, the more useless the Internet will be.

Don't believe me? Remember when Elon Musk realized he was actually going to have to _buy_ Twitter? He panicked, he tried to get out of it. He tried to argue that [Twitter's userbase was overwhelingly bots](https://www.cnn.com/2022/05/16/tech/elon-musk-twitter-spam-bots-parag/index.html). He said that advertisers had no interest in buying ads on a website mostly used by robots instead of people. He said this was "fundamental to the financial health of Twitter."

Then, as soon as he did buy Twitter, he set up [a dedicated team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XAI_(company)) whose sole purpose was making the most convincing chatbot ever made. He made it easier than ever to crowd his website with robots instead of people. Why would he do this?

Meta is likewise pouring resources into AI tools that make their flagship products, Instagram and Facebook, less useful by the day. Mark Zuckerberg has always said that the purpose of Facebook is to bring people together; but now that I am recommended posts every day written without the direct involvement of a human being, how can it accomplish that? Why would I open Instagram to see photos of my friends doing things they've never done, videos of celebrities in places they've never been, seeing advertisements for products that have never existed?

Speaking of products that have never existed, Amazon is already brimming with scams, fake flash drives and fake robot dogs, sometimes using real pictures of other people's products and sometimes using generative AI. How much worse will this problem become when the entire process can become automated? What kind of quality control can they impose when every process can be automated, every kind of proof fabricated? Who will shop on Amazon when nothing Amazon sells gets delivered?

Photographs can always be faked. They have been faked since the invention of photography. Even bad fakes will always fool someone, convince someone who wants to be convinced. But even bad fakes required _some_ effort, required _some_ reputational risk to their inventors. Now fake photographs, fakes videos, will be infinitely convincing and infinitely available, anonymously.

There is a very old expression: ["A lie can travel halfway around the world before the truth can pull its boots on."](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2014/07/13/truth/). This isn't a saying about technology, it's a saying about the human brain. A receptive brain will accept a pleasing lie that you can't dislodge with ten times, one hundred times the effort it took to put it there. This has always been a problem, but it is going to get worse.

AI is "just a tool," say its advocates. It is. You can do good things with it and bad things with it. I think the evidence is all around us (if you couldn't have predicted it ahead of time) that people overwhelmingly choose to do bad things with it. They choose to poison the information ecosystem, to lie or to recklessly disregard the truth, to carry on useless conversations they couldn't be bothered to participate in personally. To waste our time.

It doesn't matter what _you_ do. You may steadfastly refuse to use AI; I think this is fine. You will not be left behind, for what is coming for us will not spare the early adopters. You may use AI for only some small subset of things you think are morally permissible. You can try to prevent data centers from being built. You can hope against hope that the government of the United States---_this_ government of the United States!---will do something about it. Even if they stopped all AI development in the United States tomorrow, what would this accomplish? Do you think China and Russia would not be able to ruin the Internet all on their own? They have been doing a wonderful job so far.

For most of human history, the best defense against lying has been that lying well is _hard_. It is now not even a skill at all. We have invented an automatic lying machine, whose only goal is _sound_ correct. It will take anything you give it and return it to you wrapped in plausibility. Even as newer (paid, naturally) versions of these language models become available with more reliable fact-checking, the unchecked, hallucinatory models will remain available, likely forever. As the technology improves and becomes more widely understood, it will be possible to run perfectly adequate large language models locally, on your devices, for free. AI evangelists like to say that this is the worst it will ever be—but these worse versions will be available forever. They will not be replaced by the improved versions, they will just have to compete with them. Because they are less resource-intensive, and therefore cheaper, and _because_ they can lie, _because_ they are so sycophantic, they will always have a competitive advantage for some people.

But it does not stop at our screens. Not just everything we see _on_ a computer will be poisoned with justified and irremediable doubt, but everything we see that can be made _by_ a computer. Video on broadcast television, books printed by electronic printers, newspapers and magazines, vinyl for sale at the record store. Anything new, anything in shrink wrap, anything made in a factory, could have been faked.

For many things, you maybe think this doesn't matter. Maybe you're right. But for some things, and the most _important_ things, it absolutely matters. Even if some people walk the narrow path and use AI only to accomplish responsible goals responsibly, the vast majority of people will still use it do irresponsible things. The only defense against the torrent of non-information coming our way is to turn off the faucet it comes through, to learn to disregard the Internet entirely. From its very beginning, the Internet has been touted as a place to share information; for better or for worse, that's what's it's been. Soon, it will stop being that. Soon, mass media itself will stop being a source of information of any kind. It will become impossible for even the most discriminating consumer to distinguish truth from lies algorithmically designed to be as convincing as possible. In our complex world, we rely on trusted subject-matter experts to help us evaluate new evidence. When even the experts themselves can be faked, who _can_ we trust?

We have uninvented the printing press as surely as if we had burned Gutenberg's shop. Books will be available in uncounted number, but they will be meaningless. Even the small percentage of useful books will be buried under the mass of garbage competing for their attention. We will have turned the digital world and everything that depends on it into Borges's Library of Babel. You can waste your life in search of the one or two books with something real in them—but how could you tell?—or you can walk away.

Walk away, into the culture we had _before_ the printing press: stories told around the fire, caravans of traveling actors, music performed and composed not by professional virtuoso but by people in your community. The world has been shrinking for centuries, but in one respect it is going to grow again very rapidly. The importance of strangers, of people you have never met, will evaporate. For all intents and purposes, they will no longer _be_ people.

But there is one exception. Old things will still have value. Newly-produced AI slop will drown out well-done AI media as well as human-produced media, but things made _before_ the AI watershed—before you became unable to tell the difference—will retain their usefulness. Old movies, old music, and old books will become, perhaps, more important than ever. And mass-market paperbacks will still be cheap and convenient and for sale in my store.
