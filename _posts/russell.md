# Stepping Out

For some reason, as a reviewer I struggle to justify giving my opinion on a book *merely* by virtue of my having read it. I need some *angle*, some special reason for bringing book under the microscope, and for my being the person to do it. Hence the organization of the reviews in this newsletter under *series*. Already I have [delivered a review](https://open.substack.com/pub/sillywackyzanybooks/p/novelization-and-its-discontents?r=99coh&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web) for *Novelization and Its Discontents*, and another is in progress. I have ideas for a few different series that should cover a lot of my ordinary reading, should encourage me to tackle most unusual parts of my TBR, and will provide me and my ostensible readers with a framework for considering the books under question.

Today I will start a new such series, which I call *Stepping Out* for lack of a better title. In it, I will review more obscure books by otherwise popular authors, books that are outside their traditional genres. If you can think of a better title for this series, drop a suggestion in the comments.

We'll be starting off with what the cover of my US first edition proudly announces is "Bertrand Russell's First Book of Fiction," *Satan in the Suburbs*. Page numbers are from this 1953 Simon and Schuster hardcover, recently acquired from Minneapolis's coolest bookstore, [The Book House](https://www.bookhouseindinkytown.com/). It is not for sale because I am going to keep it.

## A New Departure

> To attempt a new departure at the age of eighty is perhaps unusual, though not unprecedented: Hobbes was older when he wrote his autobiography in Latin hexameters. Nevertheless, a few words may be in order, to mitigate any surprise that may be felt. I do not think that the reader's surprise to find me attempting stories can be greater than my own. *(Page viii)*

So Russell begins the book with a preface explaining himself. Bertrand Russell, if you don't know him, was an English philosopher who is undoubtedly one of the most influential people with that job description in the 20th century. He wrote for over sixty years about almost every topic in philosophy, allegedly explaining to Brand Blanshard that he neglected aesthetics only out of ignorance of art. He claims the same ignorance here: "I am incapable of critical judgment in this field, and I do not know whether the stories have any value."

I am familiar with Russell, and a big fan of his. I have read his essays "Why I Am Not a Christian" and "In Praise of Idleness", his book *The Conquest of Happiness*, and his book of letters to the public, *Dear Bertrand Russell*, and I have never read anything that would make me quail at endorsing him in mixed company. But can he write a story?

Yes and no. The stories are fundamentally silly, and his narrators and characters all speak with the same wry, understated tone of his more informal nonfiction writing, unless they lapse into deliberately exaggerated melodrama. They are not intended as serious literature, but they are also not intended as philosophical thought experiments (though some of them are that, in some places):

> I should be very sorry if it were supposed that the stories are meant to point a moral or illustrate a doctrine. Each of them was written for its own sake, simply as a story, and if it is found either interesting or amusing it has served its purpose.

All of them *are* amusing, and most are interesting - some by design, and some, perhaps, by accident.

There are five stories in the collection: the titular "Satan in the Suburbs", "The Corsican Ordeal of Miss X", "The Infra-redioscope", "The Guardians of Parnassus", and "Benefit of the Clergy". At 59 pages, "Satan in the Suburbs" is almost half of the book; the shortest, at 13 pages, is "The Guardians of Parnassus." Each story is narrated in the first person by an unnamed character, but in at least some of stories, the person is not intended to be Russell.

I will briefly summarize three of the stories, and go into greater detail on two of the others. Spoilers for any stories will be light, and only those necessary for review.

"Satan in the Suburbs" is the most explicitly philosophical. It tells the story of a Dr Mallako, who sets out an advertisement for "Horrors Manufactured Here", which turns out to be bad advice in the form of stories of people who surrendered to temptation and did immoral things for personal gain. When his clients take the advice, things go wrong and they fall to ruin. For being the longest it does not stand out as the best. It is does not take long for the reader to figure out the pattern for what inevitably happens to Mallako's clients, and this pattern is not varied upon enough to still feel fresh by the story's conclusion.

"The Infra-Redioscope" will have a familiar premise to anyone familiar with the plagiarism controversy involving Alan Moore's graphic novel *Watchmen* and the 1963 *Outer Limits* episode ["The Architects of Fear"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Architects_of_Fear). Both stories involve faking an alien invasion in order to bring about world peace; this story involves faking an alien invasion to establish economic supremacy. World peace does briefly come about, but only as a side effect, and (putting paid to the conceit of the other two stories), world peace is destroyed as soon as the deception is (inevitably) discovered. There is one additional point at the end that I will not spoil.

Finally, "The Benefit of the Clergy" is a discursive romance about an ambitious young woman who resents the domestic life imposed upon her by her traditionalist father. She woos, marries, has affairs, sets out on her own, and has an unexpectedly happy ending just as her father dies. It is the weakest story, as meandering as much as "Corsican Ordeal" but never as funny.

"The Guardians of Parnassus" is my favorite, a quiet morality tale with the structure of an MR James ghost story. It has all the hallmarks of a James tale: a narrator telling us a story that was told to him by another; the rustic setting of university life; the gradual revelation of backstory through additional indirect narrators; the final ["Jamesian wallop"](https://www.mrjamespodcast.com/) when the whole mystery of the situation is revealed in death and tragedy (although without furry monsters or wet mouths). There is no supernatural element to the story, but it is the most serious and somber of the collection, and this reinforces the ghost story construction.

The story opens with the narrator remarking that, in troubling times, people have a tendency to romanticize the past, but that stories from the past can reveal the people who lived then had their own problems. The rest of the stories continues as narrated by his father, who was "already an old man" when was born (unlike Russell's real father, who was only 30), and is telling a story about his own youth. As an undergraduate at "Oxbridge", he would often meet a mysterious, silent man and his daughter. He eventually learns that the man entered a pact, when up for election to a prestigious post at the University that would allow him to marry, with his opponent such that they would vote for each other. When the vote came, he was found to have won by one vote, and an interview with each fellow seemed to confirm he must have broken the pact and voted for himself. For this supposed treachery, he was [sent to Coventry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Send_to_Coventry) by his colleagues, a practice they maintained for his entire adult life, extending even to his wife. His daughter, though technically excluded from the ban, refused to speak to anyone who perpetuated it.

Despite Russell's warning not to look for morals in these stories, there is explicit ethical commentary in it. At the beginning, the first narrator warns against nostalgia and says this story has "helped to reconcile me to my own time." He is clearly passing a moral judgment on what has happened. Later, his father-as-narrator makes several wry remarks about the morality of ostracism: "I was young in those days, and had not the stern devotion to moral principles which enables men to inflict torture without compunction. The story shocked me, not because of the old man's sin, but because of the concerted cruelty of the whole Oxbridge community \[...\]I brooded on this situation until it was in danger of undermining my ethical convictions. I came almost to doubt whether punishment of sin is the main duty of the virtuous man." *(Pages 120 - 121)*.

A reader of the story would be no less surprised than a reader of my summary to learn that the man did not, in fact, vote for himself, but the proper resolution of the mystery, though not at all guessable, is surprising and thought-provoking enough that I will not spoil it here (except on request). Suffice it to say, the ending is grim and further reflection on the moral hazards of self-righteousness. Every story inevitably *reflects* Russell's worldview and beliefs, but this one certainly *expounds* them.

The only remaining mystery is the title: I suppose the guardians must be the "whole Oxbridge community," or any sort of high-handed moralist busybody, but what have they to do with [Parnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Parnassus), which is usually metaphorically understood as home of the Muses? If you have light to shed on this matter, please let me know in the comments.

"The Corsican Ordeal of Miss X" is the most baffling of the stories, an elaborate satire of the nobility to which Bertrand, Lord Russell himself belonged, that transforms by the end into a political thriller. Ruseell writes in the preface he wanted to "combine the moods of *[Zuleika Dobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuleika_Dobson)* and *[The Mysteries of Udolpho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysteries_of_Udolpho)*," but not having received the same classical education as Russell, I have read neither book and cannot guess about their moods, but this story does not differ dramatically in that respect from the others in this volume.

Our narrator describes meeting a friend of his and discovering his secretary, Miss X, upon returning from a vacation to Corsica has become sullen and reserved. She refuses to explain why, but overcome with compassion for his worried friend, he decides to find out in his own way. The narrator approaches an acquaintance who is of Corsican descent, and she recommends he speak with a Polish General Prz. Thus is set in motion the bulk of the story, where the narrator is shuffled from Tinker to Evers to Chance, each person getting him slightly closer to the true story what befall Miss X and how it may be remedied. Each encounter takes several pages to play out, and the purpose of each is to lampoon the sort of person each character represents: an aging society dame, an officious nationalist, the idle aristocrat. Perhaps to someone who knows such types, as Russell undoubtedly did, these caricatures are daringly effective, but to the general reader they come off as so much comic business and get rather in the way of uncovering the mystery - and when it is uncovered, the general reader might wonder what exactly the society portraits had to do with what was "really" going on. Miss X inadvertantly stumbles upon a plot to assassinate all the great Heads of State, and what comes thereafter I shall not say. The copy on the front flap of the dustjacket says that when this story was published without an author, and readers were offered a prize to guess who really wrote it. I am not surprised they did not guess Russell, but I must say I am surprised that they gussed "virtually every famous English writer \[...\], living or dead, from Galsworthy, Shaw, and Wells to Maugham and Noel Coward." Russell's prose is sometimes as funny as Coward and his viewpoint is often Wellsian, but everyone one on that list would have taken better care of the story.

As a prose stylist, Russell writes non-fiction with clarity and humor, but his style is sometimes redundant for fiction: when deciding whether to a book an appointment with Dr. Mallako, the narrator debated "whether I should seek further information at the cost of ten guineas, or should keep this sum to be spent on some other pleasure." What else does Russell suppose might motivate someone not to spend money? Later in the same story, another character explains how she was seduced into treachery and murder by the same man, by telling her a suggestive story of a woman who got what she wanted by betraying her husband. Later, she is put in a very similar position as the protagonist of Mallako's anecdote:

> Mr. Quantox seemed to hesitate, and then, at last, to make up his mind. With a momentary pang of terror she heard him repeat almost literally the words from Dr. Mallako's "little story". *(Page 45)*.

Russell nevertheless shows us the ensuing conversation between them, which does indeed match the one described in the earlier story.

An older woman cynically speculates on the motivations of a male visitor:

> "And what," she says, "brings you here? Do not pretend that it is my charms. The day for such pretense is past. For ten years it would have been true; for another ten I should have believed it. Now it is neither true nor do I believe it." *(Page 62).*

Perhaps this level of explicitness is valuable in writing philosophy, but it is clumsy in fiction - even (or perhaps especially) in light and humorous mode in which Russell is operating.

But on the whole, I find his fiction a stylistic success. His detached and understated narration is sometimes Wodehousian:

> I had nothing against fishes, which, so far as I then knew, and so far as I had observed in aquariums, were harmless and pleasant creatures, not infrequently beautiful, and possessed of a far from human dexterity in the avoidance of impacts with each other. *(Page 49)*

> Sir Bulbus and Sir Publius had had some doubts about his admission, but their doubts had been overruled by Sir Theophilus. Their doubts were not unreasonable. In the first place, unlike themselves, he had not been honored with a knighthood. There were, however, even graver objection to him. *(Page 90)*

> \[The village's\] only connection to the outside world at that time, some fifty years since, was a bus which ran three times a week to Quycombe Parva, a much larger village, with a railway station from which (it was said) persons of sufficient longevity might hope to reach Liverpool Street. *(Pages 132 - 133)*

But the British always complain about British Rail. They don't know how good they have it.

I encountered a new word, on page 124: *tergiversation*, which Merriam-Webster identifes as a near-synonym of *equivocation* that can apply to action as well as word. Etymonline gives this etymology: \"turn one\'s back on, evade,\" from *tergum* \"the back\" (a word of unknown origin) + *versare* \"to spin, turn.\"
