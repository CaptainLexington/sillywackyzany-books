I can't think of any *great book* that was straightforwardly adapted into a *great film*. Francis Ford Coppola called Mario Puzo's novel *The Godfather* "pretty cheap stuff." *MASH: A Novel About Three Army Doctors* by Richard Hooker (did you know there was a book?) is a footnote to Robert Altman's film and its subsequent 11-season television adaptation. The Altman film completely overshadowed Mike Nichols's own *Catch-22* (did you know there was a movie?) from the same year, based on Joseph Heller's 1961 novel now considered one of the great works of 20th century literature.

*Jaws* by Steven Spielberg is considered a great film; *Jaws* by Peter Benchley is an airport book. IMDb's famous Top-100 is perennially topped by *The Shawshank Redemption*, based on a [short story many people don't know is by Stephen King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rita_Hayworth_and_Shawshank_Redemption). The Wikipedia article about the novella is 447 words long: not long enough for a high school essay, and 92 of those words are about the movie. The movie's article is 8,805 words long, almost enough for an undergraduate thesis. Granted, yes, perhaps the novella is underappreciated, and perhaps the movie is overrated, but the fact that one is considered legendary and the other is considered an interesting way to spend an afternoon isn't *no* data.

Russian director Andrei Tarkosvky's films *Stalker* (based on *Roadside Picnic* by Boris and Arkady Strugatsky) and *Solaris* (based on the novel of the same name by Stanislaw Lem) are often considered great films, as the books are often considered great books---but often not by the same people. Lem didn't care for the changes Tarkovsky made to *Solaris*, and people who like both tend to say they must be appreciated separately. David Lynch's *Dune* is an amusing, if uncharacteristic, adventure film, but it is a bad adaptation of Herbert's novel. Denis Villeneuve's own somber, glossy, and faithful adaptation will have to be finished before we can tell whether it is any good or not.

Ken Kesey's *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest* became Milos Forman's, and both are great; but Forman took out everything Kesey thought most important, and Kesey repudiated the film despite its acclaim (and the book is, indeed, better).

*The Postman Always Rings Twice* and *Double Indemnity* are two novellas by James M. Cain that tell basically the same story; both were turned into films. Who decided that *The Postman Always Rings* belongs in the Modern Library's list of 100 Greatest Novels and that *Double Indemnity* belonged on AFI's 100 Years...100 Movies? I'm not sure, but having read both books and seen both movies, I can say they were chosen correctly.
