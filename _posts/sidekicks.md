On Whatnot yesterday I participated in a raid train about crime fiction; the second half of the raid train continues today. My show [on Monday](https://www.whatnot.com/live/4828d16e-8584-4975-a135-2bf67b4f1441) is about American hardboiled fiction. Today, I thought I'd share a hypothesis I've long held about why sidekicks, and sidekick narrators specifically, are so common in detective fiction.

I usually hear one of two suggestions about why detectives have sidekicks. The first is that, because Sherlock Holmes had Watson, it became a cliché. Captain Hastings and Archie Goodwin are simply made in the image of the good Doctor, because the authors who created them couldn't think of anything original. With permission, I will dismiss this out of hand. For one thing, it seems unfairly derisive of the skill of mystery writers. For another, sidekicks *per se* are not actually as common in detective fiction as they seem. Poirot's Hastings and Nero Wolfe's Archie were the first to come to mind, but Wikipedia's [list of fictional detective teams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_detective_teams) did not really reveal any more obvious examples from stories I'd actually read. In fact, even Hastings is the narrator in only a minority of Poirot's novels, despite the character's ubiquity in the television adaptation. So it seems almost no one (probably just [E. W. Hornung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Raffles_(character))) put their sidekick in just to be "the Watson." But *none* of Poirot's novels are narrated by Poirot ([although two stories are](http://www.poirot.us/narrators.php)): when Hastings is absent, the narrator is another of his friends, or someone involved in the periphery of the case. For the purposes of this discussion, I will refer to these characters as "sidekicks" even if they aren't in the traditional Watsonian role.

This fact leads people to suggest the second explanation, that sidekick narrators are there primarily for information control - if the detective were narrating, then as soon as they found important clues and made crucial deductions, we as the readers would know the solution before the author would want us to. This explanation is superficially more plausible, and in some sense true: I would not deny that sidekick narrators *do serve* this purpose on occasion, and it is used as a crutch in less successful stories. But like the ostensible ubiquity of the Watsonian sidekick, this trick is not, I think, nearly as common as people think. It certainly violates [Fair Play](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/FairPlayWhodunnit), something most British crime writers took fairly seriously. It is also not, by any means, the only way for even a well-informed narrator to conceal or omit information from the reader, if that is the desired effect (the second time in this essay I'd like to refer to a famous Agatha Christie novel, but cannot for spoiler reasons). Like the cliché argument, this argument demeans the creativity of these authors - as if all of these novels are structured just to trick the reader into being unable to solve the mystery. I don't buy it.

Instead, I believe the answer has to do with status.

If you didn't watch this excellent and illuminating video, a mere 10 minutes long, I'll excerpt the most relevant part, where he defines status for the purposes of his analysis:

> Status is about how we perceive the power or influence a person has, how someone is able to move or affect others. Do they fail to affect others, what we'll call *lower status*, or do they succeed in affecting others - *higher status*? In turn, it's about how they are moved or affected. Are they more easily moved - *lower status* - or less easily moved - *higher status*?

I believe that the reason detectives are so seldom the narrators of their own stories is that **high status people make bad point-of-view characters**. Not *protagonists*, but point-of-view characters. Sherlock Holmes is *extremely* high status - he is extremely competent in many domains, he is fairly indifferent to other people's opinions of him, he always has the upper hand, and he is himself fully aware of how high-status he is. This does not, *contra* bargain-basement writing advice, make him a boring character - he is, in fact, a fascinating character - but it can make him hard to *like*. Indeed, when we meet Holmes, it is in precisely this context: a mutual friend tells Watson Holmes is having difficulty looking for a roommate because nobody wants to live with him. Holmes's prickly nature was distorted beyond recognition by Benedict Cumberbatch's [now-infamous](https://youtu.be/LkoGBOs5ecM?si=BmoPHzut_oHs903z) portrayal, but even in its original, more sedate manifestation, it gave the Great Detective a perhaps-undeserved reputation for being a dick.

For the mysteries to be solved, we need Holmes to show off his stuff, but if he addressed us, the readers, directly, he would sound like a unbearable braggart (as he often *does* sound to criminals, clients, and policemen). We need Watson, then, not as a fool or a foil or an amateur, to be patiently explained to, but as an admirer, to paint Holmes realistically *and* flatteringly, so that we can be impressed *along with Watson* instead of *at Holmes*. When Watson tells us how Holmes outsmarted Scotland Yard and a major criminal organization, that's just a cool guy telling us how much he likes his friend. When Holmes tell us, that's a random stranger telling us how stupid everyone else he's ever met is. That's a big difference.[1](#footnote-1){#footnote-anchor-1 .footnote-anchor component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" target="_self"}

Holmes is, in fact, the narrator of [two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Adventure_of_the_Lion%27s_Mane) [stories](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/The_Adventure_of_the_Blanched_Soldier) in the Doyle canon, and incidentally they are routinely considered among the series' worst. Nicholas Meyer has Watson refer to "The Adventure of the Lion's Mane" as "drivel" in his own Holmes novel, *The Seven Per-Cent Solution*. Of the two Poirot-narrated stories mentioned above, one of them, "The Chocolate Box", has Poirot tell Hastings about the one time he *failed* as a detective: that is to say, he told the one story in which he was *low* status.

Of course, the status of characters in a story does not need to bear close relation to their social standing in the fictional world of the story; Bertie Wooster is the narrator of the Jeeves stories because, although he is technically Jeeves's employer and social superior, he is completely incompetent - he cannot move others and is easily moved himself. Jeeves, though nominally a servant, always has the upper hand. His understated wit is a tonic to Bertie's flippancy, but it would become unbearable as the narrative voice. It would feel too much like punching down.

Now, you might say, aren't a lot of detective stories actually narrated by the detectives? In the American hardboiled tradition, they certainly are. Philip Marlowe narrates his own stories, but the hardboiled private eye is not in the same situation as the genius Consulting Detective. The private eye doesn't dazzle everyone by feats of ingenious ratiocination - Raymond Chandler was [very clear about that](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Simple_Art_of_Murder) - but rather wins through by sheer determination. Marlowe and other hardboiled dicks are *not* high status - they are constantly pushed around by cops, by *femmes fatales,* by even low-ranking hoods with just one piece of valuable information, and sometimes by other detectives. When Marlowe wins out - and he doesn't always do so - it's because he holds onto his virtue long enough to see him through to the end, and when he does, he's probably lost something of value to get there.

If there is an example of a high-status detective narrator, it is Mickey Spillane's two-fisted detective Mike Hammer, on which subject I can only say that Mickey Spillane is considered one of the worst hacks of all time. The high-status narrator who is never challenged and always wins makes, at best, for a good power fantasy. It makes for bad literature.
