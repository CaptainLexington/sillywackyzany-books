In his 1930 essay "[Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren](http://www.econ.yale.edu/smith/econ116a/keynes1.pdf)", economist John Maynard Keynes predicted that within the next hundred years - just five years from the time of writing this newsletter - "the economic problem" will "be solved, or at least be within sight of solution", by which he meant that our immediate material needs will be reliably produced at above the rate they are consumed with such efficiency that for most people labor will no longer be the primary occupation of their time. He doesn't have a strong prediction about how much work will be necessary, but he supposes that three-hour shifts, five days a week will be kept around just to satisfy our perverse desire to work.

In his 1932 essay "[In Praise of Idleness](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/)," friend of the Substack Bertrand Russell opines that, in fact, production was *already at that time* sufficient for the industrial workforce to have four-hour shifts, and only a collective societal confusion as to what the hell else we could do with our time maintained the status quo. Further, he writes:

> I want to say, in all seriousness, that a great deal of harm is being done in the modern world by the belief in the virtuousness of *work,* and that the road to happiness and prosperity lies in an organized diminution of work.

I have to say, I have always agreed with him. You could ask me at any time for a half a dozen of history's greatest quotes about the folly and futility of work.

Technology has advanced more in the intervening century than either Keynes or Russell might have imagined, and I think history has actually borne them out. There is indeed, now, enough food, enough clothing, enough shelter, and enough medicine for everyone in the world, and all the deprivation and suffering of poverty that remains is the result of our inability to properly allocate the resources thus produced.

I hardly need to say that we can only expect this trend to continue, and that labor will become increasingly more productive and the things we need increasingly more abundant.[1](#footnote-1){#footnote-anchor-1 .footnote-anchor component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" target="_self"} To continue with this essay, I want you to imagine two impossible-sounding things: first, that we solve the problem of resource allocation mentioned above, and second that we commit ourselves to Russell's project: the organized diminution of work.

Keynes expresses a common worry about this:

> Yet there is no country and no people, I think, who can look forward to the age of leisure and of abundance without a dread. For we have been trained too long to strive and not to enjoy. It is a fearful problem for the ordinary person, with no special talents, to occupy himself, especially if he no longer has roots in the soil or in custom or in the beloved conventions of a traditional society. To judge from the behaviour and the achievements of the wealthy classes to-day in any quarter of the world, the outlook is very depressing!

Russell (himself a member of that wealthy class to which Keynes alludes) acknowledges the problem:

> It will be said that while a little leisure is pleasant, men would not know how to fill their days if they had only four hours' work out of the twenty-four. In so far as this is true in the modern world it is a condemnation of our civilization; it would not have been true at any earlier period.

It also cannot escape our notice that people today *do* have a great deal of leisure, and yet it is not yet making us any happier.

I believe one person can tell us why we are not happy now, and another can answer the question of how we *can* be happy when the Keynesian age of leisure and abundance finally comes: the anthropologist Robin Dunbar, and the philosopher Bernard Suits.
