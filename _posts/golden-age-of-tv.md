Last year, when I wrote most of the text of this post, I had just read two books about television production: Warren Littlefield's *Top of the Rock: The Rise and Fall of Must-See TV*, and *Directed By James Burrows* by the man himself. I was inspired to read Littlefield because I changed my Whatnot schedule to 7:30 on Thursday nights, a time smack dab in the middle of NBC's old Night of Bests (in Central Time, at least). I used old Must-See TV promotional images for the first two show thumbnails, but it seems there are not enough of them on the Internet to make it a habit of it.

At this point I must make a confession: I love television. I know this is a newsletter originally, and perhaps primarily, about books. I know you are here for discussion of books. I am (usually) here to provide it. I love to read, and to talk about books, but the truth is I love television more. I apologize to the hand-wringing parents of the 1960s who were worried that this exact thing would happen. I am glad I don't have to choose, but if I did, I would choose television in a heartbeat. But since I don't, I read and watch television, and I read books about television. (Please give me your recommendations for television about books).

Now, are these books good? Neither is intended as a work of literature, so reviews are worth only so much. If you want the professional insight Littlefield and Burrows have to offer, you have no choice but to read the books whether they are well-written or not. *Top of the Rock* tells the story of Littlefield's meteoric rise in and abrupt departure from NBC's entertainment division, from the early '80s under Brandon Tartikoff to his dismissal by Don Ohlmeyer in 1998. It comprises mostly of interviews with studio figures and creatives who worked with Littlefield on the impressive roster of shows he was involved in. There are chapters on *Cheers*, *The Cosby Show*, *Frasier,* *Will & Grace*, *E.R.*, and more. I listened to the audiobook read by Bob Balaban, which made it occasionally difficult to remember which interviewee was speaking, but it was worth it [for the memes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dvnA8Vtf3rA&t=102s).

My biggest criticism is that the chapters are arranged by series, or sometimes by subject, like Littlefield's odious boss Don Ohlmeyer, when the book should have been structured chronologically, probably by broadcast season. The chapter on *Cheers* ends in 1993, and should logically lead into the development of *Frasier*, but we jump *back* in time to the development of *The Cosby Show* instead. Organizing the entire book through the chronology of Littlefield's career would have been less confusing for that reason, but it would also have been more insightful as we see how one show's performance influences decisions on another show (something that happens several times in the book, but you have to squint to see it), and would have had better dramatic tension as Brandon Tartikoff leaves and Ohlmeyer descends into alcoholism and boorishness. That the *Will & Grace* chapter goes a few years past Littlefield's tenure seems a minor problem compared to the clunky structure of the book we got. Littlefield had a co-writer, T.R. Pearson, so we cannot blame this defect of the book on Littlefield's outsider status.

The same is not true of *Directed By James Burrows*. Burrows, the most famous, prolific, and successful sitcom director in television history, is the sole credit on the cover, and if he *had* a ghostwriter, I am not surprised they wouldn't want to be credited. The book is a *mess*. Burrows comes off as compassionate and professional, but his sentences, his paragraphs, and even his chapters wander off subject like he's a stereotypical old man getting distracted in the middle of a story. Burrows *is* an old man, of course, but there ought to have been an editor somewhere along the way to clean this up. It reads like it was dictated---dictated, as the old note goes, but not read. It is worth reading if you like old sitcoms, but it is not, strictly speaking, a good *book*.

But in addition to opinions about the books themselves, these two writers solidified in me an opinion about television:

## The 1990s was the Golden Age of Television.

By this, I mean two things: first, the '90s was a better decade than any prior decade in terms of television; and secondly, the three decades \_after\_ the '90s *aren't television*.

I don't feel like I need to defend the greatness of the '90s too hard: in one week of network television in Fall 1996, you could watch *Seinfeld, Frasier,* *The X-Files*, *Friends,* *The Simpsons* (season 7! of *the Simpsons*!), *E.R.*, *Law & Order*, *Malcolm in the Middle*, *Home Improvement*, both *Voyager* **and** * Deep Space Nine (*and *Babylon 5), Buffy the Vampire Slayer*, and *Everybody Loves Raymond*. I don't love or even like every one of those shows, but every one of them is legendary. You didn't have to Google any of them.

I don't mean to denigrate the shows of the earlier decades: there was certainly good and great television in every decade from the 1950s to 2000. I think we got better every year. It makes sense that the '90s was better than the '80s, because we had 10 years more practice; and in the '80s, we had 10 years more practice than in the '70s. I think this is more or less how all art, all science, all culture works, but sometimes we get caught up in unproductive trends that cause local slumps that *seem* like an overall decline. This an illusion. We get better every year.

But wait! you say. In the 2000s, we had *ten years more* practice than we did in '90s, and in the 2010s ten years further! What gives? By your logic, the 2000s and the 2010s should be even better than the '90s.

You're right, they should be, and they were. But they weren't television. "[Peak TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age_of_Television_(2000s–present))" isn't the Golden Age of Television, it's

## The Movie Serial Renaissance

Back in the studio Hollywood days, you'd go to the picture palace in your small town square, give a 16-year-old girl a nickel, and sit down for something like five hours: first a newsreel, then a cartoon, then a serial, then a double feature (with dancing hot dogs at the intermission).

The serials were episodic: the installment playing in the cinema would be a continuation of a previous installment, or be continued in the next installment, or usually both. The theater would get the latest installment every week, and you'd have to go back every week to keep up with the story. Installments usually ended on cliffhanger, and opened with recaps of the previous episode.

Episode-end cliffhangers are *de rigeur* in Peak TV, because streaming (and before that, [time-shifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_shifting)) make it possible for producers to assume viewers have seen every prior episode. During the 50 years in which broadcast was the *only* kind of television, time-shifting was accessible only to the nerds who knew how to program their VCRs, and tapes were expensive enough that even these people usually only did it for big events, like sports games or season finales. In this era, as Burrows writes, "we had to pretend that people were tuning in for the first time." *(Chapter 8: Cheers, Part II).* Network television didn't build an audience with plot-shaking cliff-hangers that kept people tuning back in; they built them with comfortable *status quo* stories that wouldn't *alienate* audiences who missed a week, which was virtually inevitable at that time.

Peak TV doesn't follow this formula; even its successful comedies, like *The Good Place* and *Schitt's Creek*, have season- and series-long plots that require watching every episode. Peak TV isn't the culmination of five decades of lessons of television: it's a revival of the movie serial. They haven't learned from 50 years of television production: they *discarded* 50 years of television production, embraced an older model, and updated it for modern tastes.

Of course, it's not an instantaneous transition: even with time-shifting, network TV is still pretty reliant on the old episodic model---and pretty much none of their shows are considered part of the Golden Age. But wait again! you say again. So why isn't *network* TV now even better than the '90s? They still have decades of experience to draw from. Littlefield thinks NBC lost track of the creator-respecting culture he fostered during his tenure. He quotes Bob Broder in chapter 16 of *Top of the Rock*: "The allegiance, the belief in talent, kept people like Jimmy Burrows hanging around to work with them. Today at NBC, they have no belief in talent, no relationship with the creative community, and they have too many people doing the job."

I think this is partly true, but I think that's caused by a shortage of money. Networks don't get the ratings, and therefore, the advertising revenue, that they used to. They are all parts of conglomerates that make their money in other industries, or through other venues. Anybody who wants to make good work in television goes to a streamer, and networks can't afford to lure them back.

## So what?

I don't actually have a problem with the name "Golden Age of Television," and even if I did I know better than to try to change a phrase with such cultural currency. I also don't dislike serial television, although I wish that we did---and perhaps even predict that we will---have just as much high-quality episodic television to choose from. I make this distinction not to tut-tut people using words in a way I don't like, or to claim that the TV that's on now isn't as good as it's cracked up to be---just to bring attention to a fundamental difference in storytelling philosophy that used to make TV special, and to lament that, when this philosophy was abandoned, people thought it was the beginning of a Golden Age.
