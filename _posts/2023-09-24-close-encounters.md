# *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*

Steven Spielberg does not usually write his own screenplays. Of the 34 feature films he's directed as of 2023, he has received screenplay credit on only four: last year's *The Fabelmans;* 1964's *Fireflight,* his \$500-dollar student film; 2001's *A.I. Artifial Intelligence*, based on a Brian Aldiss short story, adapted by Ian Watson, and heavily influenced by the late Stanley Kubrick; and this film, 1977's *Close Encounters of the Third Kind.*

This isn't because Spielberg isn't closely involved in the writing of his projects. You need only glance at [the](https://kottke.org/13/03/transcript-of-raiders-of-the-lost-ark-brainstorming-session) *[Raiders of the Lost Ark](https://kottke.org/13/03/transcript-of-raiders-of-the-lost-ark-brainstorming-session)* [brainstorming session](https://kottke.org/13/03/transcript-of-raiders-of-the-lost-ark-brainstorming-session) with George Lucas and Lawrence Kasdan to see that many of the crucial elements of the story come directly from Spielberg's mouth, even though screenplay credit went to Kasdan and story credit went to Lucas. Not that I think they don't deserve that credit: film making is collaborative art form, and the lines we draw for assigning credit are essentially arbitrary, though not totally meaningless. I assume Spielberg's influence on the screenplays he directs is often like this. But I, for one, am glad Spielberg is not the credited screenwriter on most of his movies, because I don't think the screenplay for *Close Encounters* is particularly good.

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* is essentially Spielberg's idea, but in its original form that idea was very vague: he just wanted to make a movie "about" UFOs. In its earliest stages, it was just going to be documentary. According to Wikipedia, "\[t\]hough Spielberg received sole credit for the script, he was assisted by [Paul Schrader](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Paul_Schrader), [John Hill](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/John_Hill_(screenwriter)), [David Giler](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/David_Giler), [Hal Barwood](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Hal_Barwood), [Matthew Robbins](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Matthew_Robbins_(screenwriter)), and [Jerry Belson](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Jerry_Belson), all of whom contributed to the screenplay in varying degrees." It goes on to suggest these early drafts took very different approaches: the protagonist was at one point a cop; Paul Schrader's draft was described by Spielberg later as a "James Bond adventure". According to the summary of Ray Morton's *[Making of](https://www.raymorton.com/books/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-the-making-of-steven-spielbergs-classic-film/)* [book](https://www.raymorton.com/books/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-the-making-of-steven-spielbergs-classic-film/), Spielberg became dissatisfied with the various stories people had written, and decided to rewrite the script himself.

Without a copy of the book to hand, I can't estimate what parts of these earlier drafts are kept or were influential in his new draft, and even with every copy of every draft in front of me it would be impossible to sharply delineate one contributor's ideas from another's. Nevertheless, what Spielberg's finished screenplay suggests to me is that his desire to make this movie was not motivated by a desire to tell a particular story; rather, he had a feeling he wanted to go for, some specific visuals he wanted to create, and he put down on the page just enough of a story to get those ideas across. This method works for the Coen Brothers, but I don't think it works for Steven Spielberg.

Spoilers, for the film and the book, will follow.

*Note*: I watched the 2017 4K restoration of the 1998 director's cut for this review; my comments are about that version of the film. I read the 1977 hardcover Book Club Edition of the novelization; I doubt there were ever any revisions to the text subsequent to its first printing, but any page numbers cited are from that edition. That copy, if this review makes you want to own it, is [currently for sale on my eBay store](https://www.ebay.com/itm/155790616141).

## The Movie

The story of *Close Encounters* follows two threads: on the one hand, we watch Bob Balaban and Francois Truffaut zip around the world investigating places where strange things are going on, and on the other we follow Roy Neary's slow descent into obsession following his own firsthand encounter with a UFO. (There are two-and-a-half threads if you count Barry and Jillian Guiler, who live in Roy Neary's hometown and have their own encounters that converge with Roy's only later on). These threads all come together at the story's climax, when Roy and Jillian crash an orchestrated UFO landing at Devil's Tower.

When we meet Roy Neary, he's a harried, but not unhappy, family man, with a wife and three kids. He's an electrician who works for the power company; he is brought into contact with the UFOs when he is asked to investigate a blackout in the middle of the night. After his first contact, he needs more: he repeatedly returns to the spot of his first encounter, and he becomes fascinated with an intrusive image: a particular mountain he tries again and again to reproduce with increasingly inconvenient materials: shaving cream, mashed potatoes, his own backyard. This final act of creative landscaping prompts his wife to leave Roy (and the movie) with the children. We never see them again. They are not even there for Roy to wave goodbye to when he boards the ship.

There is much I like about this movie: the deliberate confusion of the Truffaut sequences, where characters speak over each other, or inaudibly, or in untranslated foreign languages, with major plot developments often happening subtly or off-screen, pulls the viewer into the mystery along with the characters; many images are striking, such as Spielberg's favorite: Barry opening the door to his house and revealing the blaze of orange light; and Dreyfuss is, as always, a charming and amiable leading man.

But I do not like the story, and my reasons boil down to one issue: why is this movie about Roy Neary? Like Indiana Jones in the above-mentioned *Raiders*, Neary does not actually influence the outcome of the plot. As with Dr. Jones, he simply arrives at the secret base in the middle of the desert just in time to watch the climax happen without him. *Raiders*, of course, is strong evidence this does not matter very much for whether a story is good, but Indiana Jones is also several things Roy Neary is not: funny, charismatic, dynamic, sexy, and smart. (Richard Dreyfuss is all of those things, but not Roy Neary). For much of the movie, Roy Neary is feeling emotions he can't explain, and I was hoping that, perhaps, the interiority of a novel might give us some clarity about his motivations.

## The Novelization

It does not.

In fact, the book does not have much interiority at all. The very first lines of the book set the tone for what is to come - literal, if flowery, descriptions of what appears on screen in the corresponding scene in the film:

> Seven misshapen figures emerged from a blinding swirl of desert sand and sage. Their images hazing in and out of tons of gushing earth. *(Page 1)*

This is also the first instance of a peculiar quirk of the narration, a tendency to separate dependent clauses into their own abortive little sentence fragments. It is clunky and never seems to be done deliberately, neither for emphasis nor for poetry. It feels more like a typo than a stylistic choice.

The first several scenes concerning Truffaut's French UFOlogist, Lacombe, and his impromptu interpreter, Balaban's Laughlin, are, as I observed above, deliberately hard to follow. Most of the dialogue is irrelevant, and so we often can't hear what is being said. What is important is that characters are shouting, they are surprised, they are concerned, they are confused. We share that confusion, and the significance of many scenes becomes clear only in retrospect. Of course, the characters speaking *do* have dialogue, even if it is in French or Spanish or drowned out by a sandstorm. In the book, we get to read all of it.

All of it.

The scene in the air traffic control tower, for instance, is not interesting because the characters have brilliant things to say. They mutter jargon into headsets and talk over each other. The scene is interesting because the blocking and the performances reveal the emotions of the characters even though their professional context forces them to remain ostensibly neutral. The average viewer probably doesn't understand a word any of the characters say, if they can even make it out, until the very end, when the tower asks the pilots whether they'd like to report a UFO sighting or not. We know this is when Spielberg expects us to pay attention to the words, because everything else is quiet for the first time in the scene.

The book could convey the tension of this moment by describing the thoughts of the characters, or, as the film does, by their body language. But apart from "Harry Crain jumped off his stool and everyone in the dim room tensed", the scene is primarily conveyed by representing every single line of the exchange between ATC and the pilots, with jargon and neutrality intact. Only an air traffic controller could find this scene interesting, and they will be sorely disappointed by the rest of the book. What is *deliberately obscured* in the movie becomes *impossible to ignore* in the book. This happens pretty much throughout the Lacombe/Laughlin sequences, and I won't touch on them again.

Roy is, as he was in the film, a bit of a cypher. According to Wikipedia, Spielberg abandoned the police officer draft of the film because he found it "hard to identify with men in uniform. I wanted to have Mr. Everyday Regular Fella.\" Roy is certainly that - he has almost no identifying characteristics. We first meet Roy, in the book as in the film, in his living room, surrounded by his children and an enormous model train set. One unambiguous improvement to this scene in the book is Roy's train-centric math lesson for his son. In the movie, he asks his son to say what fraction of the way onto a bridge a train car has to be to avoid a collision; the son has no response and the train crashes. This has nothing to do with trains or, really, with fractions. Sammy Fabelman was no doubt excited to film a model train crash for a feature film, but in the novel, Roy deals with trains and math in the obvious way:

> "Suppose," he said, "the station master assigns you eighteen cars. Then he says, 'Make up two trains with the same number of cars in them.' What do you do?" *(Page 20)*

The train crash instead comes a few moments later, when Roy interrupts his wife trying to communicate something about her inner life to show her a newly-installed revolving train bridge that malfunctions.

It is around this time, in the book and in the movie, that Roy sees *Pinocchio* is playing at a local movie theater, and is distressed to discover his children have no interest in going to see it. *Pinocchio* is, believe it or not, at the heart of this film - specifically its most famous song, Jiminy Cricket's "When You Wish Upon a Star." Spielberg said, "I hung my story on the mood the song created, the way it affected me personally.\" The song plays, in orchestral arrangement by John Williams, over the closing credits. The lyrics appear in the closing narration of the book (used, the copyright page reassures us, by permission). But *Roy Neary has no wish*. There is absolutely nothing to suggest Roy Neary is in any way dissatisfied with his current life.

Ronnie, on the other hand, has much to say in this scene she doesn't in the film. She expresses that she finds the dull routine of being a housewife and full-time mother suffocating. She tries to get Roy to understand for two full pages, but he never catches on to what she's talking about.

> "I'm not asking for a week in Acupulco. I mean I'm so starved for something to happen, I'd go bananas if you brought me a flower once. One perfect rose."
>
> Neary winced again. "I always forget that."
>
> "When you crave change the way I do," Ronnie said, "you'll settle for anything. New potholders. Going down to the Hertz office and watching them rent Pintos. Calling time and weather and dial-a-joke." *(Page 24)*

Why the hell isn't this story about *her*? If you read this scene and learned one of these characters was going to be so obsessed with UFOs that it ruins their life and their only choice is to go get on the UFO and live with the aliens, *why on Earth would you guess Roy?* If it had been Ronnie, the scene would feel overwrought; as it is, the only purpose it seems to serve is make us think Roy's family is better off without him, which I hardly take to be intentional.

Even after Roy is exposed to the aliens and becomes obsessed with them, it hardly feels like a *wish*. It is more like a compulsion. Before his fixation takes definite shape around Devil's Tower, Roy erects a platform on top of their garage and sits on it all day with a pair of binoculars (just like in the film, Roy is fired the night after the blackout for unclear reasons).

> His father let the binoculars drop to his lap. He stared down through the gathering dusk at his youngest son. Even through the kitchen window Ronnie could see that Roy's face was damp. He must have been crying behind the binoculars. *(Page 116)*

This theme is elaborated on throughout: Roy's inability to think about anything but aliens is *deeply unpleasant* to him. The aftermath of Roy's attempt to sculpt Devil's Tower in mashed potatoes is even more unhappy in the book than in the film, as he locks himself in the shower not later that night, but actually leaves the dinner table early to do so:

> The bathroom was dark. Water was pouring into the sink and the bathtub was half full, water smashing down it into it from the showerhead. Neary was huddled in the far corner of the darkness, his hands mashed over his mouth to keep the sobs inside. Ronnie turned off the sink faucets, but left the shower running.
>
> Neary tried to smile at his wife. His convulsions subsided slowly. "It's like the hiccups," he said in a small, childish voice. "I started and I can't stop. What's happening to me?" *(Page 119)*

This is Roy's reaction every time he tries to resist the intrusive thoughts of the UFOs and focus on his real life: failure, depression, despair. For the rest of the book, he is only described as happy when he makes progress towards abandoning that life and committing everything to the UFOs.

Roy doesn't understand - he never understands - what drove this compulsion, but we do. In the film and in the book, Lacombe says that dozens - perhaps hundreds - of people were "invited" to Devil's Tower by having this compulsion psychically implanted in them *by the aliens*. This is not the story of a man being granted a simple wish against all odds. It's the story of aliens who figured out the simplest way to abduct humans was to *make* them *want* to be abducted. That's an interesting idea for a horror story, but Spielberg doesn't seem to think he's writing one of those.

So much for Roy Neary. As far as the story being science fiction, there are few scientific curiosities:

I have always taken issue with *Close Encounters'*s contention that aliens would communicate using musical notes; although alien life could plausibly have a sense of hearing, they would not interpret frequencies the same way we do. There is nothing mathematically or physically interesting about the frequencies we, as Western European humans, interpret as named notes. In the book, we grapple with this by repeatedly describing the notes as slightly *off*, not *quite* the notes we'd normally expect:

> The toy xylophone was poorly tuned. That was why the five notes sounded so strange when little Barry played them.
>
> He didn't learn them all at once, Jillian noted from the other room. He had kept working them on the tune until he got...well...the way he wanted it.
>
> To Jillian's ear, even though the tune was strange, Barry's chuckles were reassuring. He was there. He was happy. The tune's curious sequence of five notes - where do kids get these ideas? - was oddly disturbing, but of course those toy xylophones were never accurate. It was easy to make them sound...well...peculiar. *(Page 89)*

I think we are meant to interpret Jillian's excuse about the toy xylophone being out of tune as a rationalization of the strangeness of the notes, because she doesn't understand that the *real* reason the notes are strange is that they are coming (through Barry) from an alien mind. But if that's the case, how *does* Barry get them to sound weird? What *can* you do to change the tuning of a toy xylophone?

When Roy and his wife attend the Air Force press conference to hear the old redneck talk about the time he saw Bigfoot, we get a little bit of extra business at the end where a distraught Roy Neary wanders into the basement and accidentally discovers the entire building's breaker panel. Consulting the diagram, he flips a few switches and "darkened some offices and turned the lights on in others. Across the entire broad face of the DAX Air Force Administration Facility, shining forth into the night to see for miles around, the windows spelled out three letters: U F O." *(Page 107).*

First, this stunt is incredibly stupid and pointless. Secondly, it is impossible to "turn the lights on" in an office from the breaker panel unless turning off power at the breaker panel was the *normal way* people turned their lights on and off. If the light switch *in the office* is off, you cannot switch a breaker any number of times to turn it back on.

When Roy and Jill arrive at the UFO landing site:

> Roy and Jill could not make out what all the equipment was for, but they did recognize some laser apparatus, biochemical instruments, devices for thermal and electromagnetic measurements, looking like bazookas on their tripods, a couple of spectrographic analyzers, and a lot of complicated-looking instruments intended to monitor and measure God only knows what. *(Page 182)*

Roy is a municipal electrical engineer, and Jill is a single mother and amateur painter. They *recognized* ("a couple of"!) spectrographic analyzers?

When the largest of the UFOs lands:

> The mass \[of the UFO\] had created its own negative gravity field, and within moments everyone and everything had become 40% weightless. This cheered everyone. They started bouncing and cavorting buoyantly in the air, some of the more athletic doing cartwheels and somersaults, hanging in the air like Dr. J, their colleagues in jumpsuits sliding and bouncing beneath them with their cameras, snapping pictures of the whole incredible thing.

Either the UFO is startlingly dense, or it has an artificial gravity field of some kind; either way, the effect of having a gravitational pull of such a significant fraction of the Earth's that objects nearby lose a full *40%* of their weight would create a lot more havoc than sending radio technicians into delirious cartwheels. Think of what the moon, at only 1.2% of the Earth's mass and much farther away, does to the ocean twice a day. About this scene, I can only say it would have been even worse on film.

Finally, before allowing him to board the UFO, Lacombe has Roy sign a few legal documents, including one that will have him declared *legally dead* after he is gone. Perhaps this is so his wife and children can make a claim on his life insurance, which *certainly* absolves Roy of any residual responsibility he may have for them.

## The Discontent

Steven Spielberg's name is on the cover of the book, but like the screenplay, it was not entirely his work. Unlike the screenplay, he wasn't happy with the final product. Wikipedia presents these comments from the 17th issue of sf fan magazine *Starlog* (emphasis mine)*:*

> It was very early on when we made the deal with [Dell](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Dell_Publishing)\... It included an advance with a promise that I was going to have direct writing input into the book. But post-production on the movie became so impossible that I had to get somebody else to write it. I didn\'t write the first, second, or third drafts. Those were written, based on my screenplay, by Leslie Waller, a very good writer. When I read his drafts, though, I told the publishers that unless it was cleaned up I wouldn\'t let my name go out with the book. **So I sat down and spent less than a week -- I wouldn\'t say rewriting the novel -- but polishing it, and taking a lot of the plot and twisting it back into the direction of the screenplay. All told, there\'s about 20 percent of me in the book. I wish I could say there was more, but there\'s not.** I cringe when I see my name on the cover, and I usually avoid it at bookstores. Actually, I\'ve never bought a copy.

To be honest, having read the book, I am not sure why he was so upset. It hews very closely to the story of the final film. I don't think there is a single scene in the movie missing from the book, and the only full scene in the book I don't remember from the movie is a brief vignette where Stevie Wonder's Moog synthesizer is mysteriously requisitioned and modified by Lacombe's team. At less than two pages, this chapter was probably only included because it was originally in Spielberg's screenplay, although I can't be sure.

There are a few minor changes, besides those I've already mentioned. Instead of driving all the way to Devil's Tower, Roy flies there and rents a car on arrival. He bumps into Jill at the Wyoming airport and they drive to Devil's Tower together, rather than finding each other in the helicopter after he identifies her as a friend in his interview with Lacombe (which he does *anyway* in the book, even though Lacombe saw them arrive together!).

The prose in the book can be amateurish, but without seeing Leslie Waller's drafts it is impossible to know who is to blame. Not having read any of his other books, but taking Spielberg at his word that Waller is a "very good writer," I'm inclined to blame instead the movie director who rewrote the entire draft in less than a week.

The book ends with an "Epilogue", which is little more than an advertisement for the Center for UFO Studies in Evanston, Illinois, written by the movie's scientific consultant, J. Allen Hynek. Like Spielberg, Hynek has a rather rosy view of what happened to Roy Neary, describing the story as a "fantasy" that might stir the reader's "imagination and curiosity." I might have said "terrifying ordeal", or "tragedy", or "parable of abuse and undiagnosed mental illness" (which is also my interpretation of *The Fabelmans*, incidentally). Hynek assures us that "the UFO Phenomenon is real," but given what they seem to think will happen, I can only be grateful it's very, very unlikely to be real in the way presented in *Close Encounters of the Third Kind.*
