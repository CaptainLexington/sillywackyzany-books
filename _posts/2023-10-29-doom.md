# DOOM: Knee Deep in the Dead

We all have our prejudices and preferences, what [Reading Glasses](https://maximumfun.org/podcasts/reading-glasses/) refers to as our wheelhouse; mine is defined more by what I *don't* read than what I *do*. I don't read pulp Westerns. I don't read contemporary romance. More than anything, I don't read military fiction, and *especially* not mil sf. The protagonist of the landmark 1993 id Software first-person shooter *DOOM* is called [Doomguy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doomguy) by most fans, but "Doom Marine" is also a common moniker. He is, ostensibly, a Space Marine, and in Brad Linaweaver and Dafydd ab Hugh's 1994 tie-in novel *Knee Deep in the Dead,* first in a series of four, they decide to really lean into this designation and he becomes USMC Corporal Flynn "Fly" Taggart. Marine jargon suffuses the book from start to finish, along with a military outlook and and a more-or-less unreflective confidence in the righteousness and effectiveness of the US military. I had to work to finish it, but that's not the only reason why.

I read the ebook edition, so quotes will chapter citations instead of page numbers.

## The Game

*DOOM* is a strange videogame to novelize, as it has almost no story. The bare bones of the plot are relayed in text screens that show up at the beginning of each of the three level packs; the rest is relayed only in [the official manual](https://www.starehry.eu/download/action3d/docs/Doom-Manual.pdf), which no one has ever read. You're on Mars, there are demons. Shoot.

Furthermore, even this gameplay itself resists adaptation. Monsters appear around corners, inside closets, in locked rooms, with no plausible explanation for their presence or their behavior. They flood into open courtyards from secret compartments to be mown down by conveniently-placed, unaccompanied human weapons. Keycards, powerups, healthpacks, and ammunition litter the world and float at eye height.

*DOOM*'s level design is famous, but it is surely famous for its playability, for its challenge and intricacy. The architecture itself is artificial and meaningless, bordering on non-representational: rooms and corridors with arbitrary and garish colors lead from one to another with no indication of what any place might be for apart from shooting monsters. Compared to near-contemporary Build Engine games like the criminally-underrated *[Blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_(video_game))*, whose first level takes us from a tomb, through a graveyard, into a church, and finally into a secret lab, *DOOM*'s levels are impossible to explain in conventional terms. They have names like "Hangar", "Nuclear Plant", and "Toxin Refinery", but there are no spacecraft in the hangar, no cooling stacks in the plant, and there are toxins in every level. Then when you get to the end of a stage, you press a button and disappear to an unrelated room in an unrelated location.

In short, *DOOM* is so relentlessly gamified, so ruthlessly optimized for fragging demons, that it has no context. There are no real people, real places, real things that could exist outside of a texture pack. Almost nothing that makes *DOOM* *DOOM* could be conveyed in a book, so what did they do?

## The Novelization

They stick *very* close to the source material. The backstory in the manual says that our space marine was sent to Mars as punishment for "assault\[ing\] a superior officer for ordering his soldiers to fire upon civilians."[1](#footnote-1){#footnote-anchor-1 .footnote-anchor component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" target="_self"} Linaweaver and ab Hugh recognize that the real Marine Corps has a different idea of punishment than "free trip to space", and court martial proceedings are under way when Fly's unit, Fox Company, is given urgent orders to come to Mars for backup. Fly's taken along because he's considered safer under their own guard, although during the mission he's left with two privates from the permanent Martian detail, anyway (both named Ron, as a "joke".) When Fox Company never returns from the mission and spooky stuff starts to go down, he mentally overpowers the Rons and begins one-man rescue mission - not of his unit, but of his "buddy" Arlene Sanders.

Arlene is a Marine in Fox Company, and Fly wants to ~~jump her bones~~ ~~marry her~~ be her friend. Arlene is tailor-made to be attractive to the straight male mil sf reader - she's tough as nails, a better soldier than Fly, looked up to by strong men, and able to stand up for herself when picked on by weaker men. She's also a science fiction nerd, referencing movies and books to normie Fly often enough that, before we meet Arlene in person, he's already uncomprehendingly repeating her references to us. ("'There is no cause so noble it will not attract fugheads;' one of those sci-fi writers Arlene is always shoving at me, David Niven or someting." Chapter 1, but don't worry, something like this happens about one a chapter). She's also drop-dead gorgeous, and, as we are assured towards the end of the book when she is *receiving emergency medical treatment*, has "ample" breasts (the free space in my "men describing boobs" Bingo Card).

Saving Arlene is Fly's only goal until, halfway through the book, they are reunited and he signs on to *her* goal, of defeating all the demons and preventing the invasion of Earth. There is no need to describe the plot or scenes in detail: it is one sequence after another of encountering demons, grabbing weapons, and combining the two to proceed. The only thing worth remarking on is how closely, far to its detriment, these encounters hew to the game itself.

We meet every major monster from the game, although from zombies, imps, and demons, the writers recognize that normal humans are unlikely to coin the extremely ~~lame~~ specific names from the game: [cacodemons](https://doom.fandom.com/wiki/Cacodemon/Doom) are "pumpkins," [lost souls](https://doom.fandom.com/wiki/Lost_Soul/Doom) are "flying skulls", [Barons of Hell](https://doom.fandom.com/wiki/Baron_of_Hell/Doom) are promoted to Hell-princes, and [cyberdemons](https://doom.fandom.com/wiki/Cyberdemon/Doom) get a retro mod to become steam-demons. [Monster infighting](https://doom.fandom.com/wiki/Monster_infighting) is canon, and in fact plays a much larger role in combat for Fly and Arlene then it ever does for the average DOOM player. Every major conflict they enter, including the final confrontation with the [spiderdemon](https://doom.fandom.com/wiki/Spiderdemon), relies on getting the monsters to fight each other so that our heroes can pick off the stragglers. These confrontations also get less detailed as the story goes on, even as they ostensibly get more challenging - there are only so many novel ways to describe shooting monsters until they are dead. The book seems to be aware of this: "All the chambers seemed more and more identical," Fly muses in Chapter 32.

They defeat the spiderdemon, and look into the sky just in time to see nuclear explosions dot the continents of Earth. The book ends on a cliffhanger as Arlene tells us she knows what to do next. Three books in the series follow. Read them if you dare.

## The Discontent

This book was written by two people, veteran tie-in novelist Dafydd ab Hugh, and [Prometheus award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus_Award) winning libertarian essayist Brad Linaweaver. I would be pretending to know too much about the writing process to try to explain any features of the book this way, but is sure *feels* like two people considering how inconsistently Fly expresses himself. Arlene is the most obvious example: throughout the book and with no apparent change over time, Fly oscillates between thinking of her as an important "buddy" (the word has lost all meaning for me), as an object of resigned and hopeless pining, and as practically already his girlfriend. He says he wants to stay buddies as late as Chapter 28, even though they shared a passionate, mutual kiss in Chapter 26. Sometimes, he thinks of Arlene as "one of the guys", a "right handsome" (as they would say in a "braver age"??, Chapter 3) "pal" who is just as tough and smart as any man. At other times, he talks about her like a nightclub comic talks about his nagging wife: when Arlene insists on calling certain monsters *imps*, Fly reflects that, "Marine or not, she was still a woman." (Chapter 17). Ladies be naming, amirite?

This ambivalence also comes out in the tension between portraying Fly as a patriotic gung-ho marine *and* as an outspoken maverick who conforms to military procedure only to the extent he thinks it is correct. In Chapter 6, he describes the pride he feels at wearing the Marine's signature minimalist hairstyle:

> The Corps is protective of its haircut, flat on the top and shaved on the sides. We're talking a sign of distinction, a challenge thrown at every other service. God help the Navy, Army, or Space Force puke who shows up on one of our bases in a high-and-tight!

Just a few pages later, in a flashback when a marine (the same CO who orders Fly to shoot on civilians) expresses sexist contempt for Arlene's ability as a soldier, suggesting *she* doesn't deserve to wear the haircut, Fly is willing to go to blows:

> But mainly I spoke up because at some deep level I hate all rules, symbols, rituals, fighting words, gang colors, routines, decorations, medals, trophies, badges... and anything else that suggests one human being is to be taken more seriously than another in a given situation simply on the basis of plumage.

Of course, these kinds of cognitive dissonance could easily exist in one person. I would not even call it unrealistic; it is a flaw in this book only because it doesn't seem to be aware of the inconsistencies.

At the risk of stating the obvious, the most bizarre thing about the book was how much it felt like a videogame - but videogames that would be released *after* DOOM, as well as DOOM itself. In Chapter 3, he uses "an instrument console marked UAC and covered with sticky-pad notes", a classic environmental storytelling cliche - but not one established until after full-3D rendering became the norm. DOOM was too low-resolution to pull off anything like this. Starting in Chapter 10, but on a few further occasions, Fly relies on stealth kills with his bayonet to take out monsters without "call\[ing\] monsters from the vasty deep."

DOOM's first several maps are scattered around a crater on Phobos, according to an interstitial overworld map, but in the novel these same places are successive levels of a single facility.

> Glancing down, I saw that the access shaft did not descend the full six stories required to reach the nuclear plant. The ladder ended in a few ragged shreds of metal; an explosion had cut off that route. (Chapter 7)

This describes several locations in *Half-Life*, but nowhere in *DOOM*.

An adaptation of a videogame *property* is not, in principle, a bad idea, but *DOOM* read like an adaptation of a playthrough of the game. Like the far superior *[DOOM](https://www.doomworld.com/10years/doomcomic/comic.php)* [comic](https://www.doomworld.com/10years/doomcomic/comic.php), the plot is linear and monotonically increasing: the protagonist serially encounters tougher enemies and bigger guns. Unlike the comic's Berserker-packin' man-and-a-half, whose bombastic self-awareness of that structure's artificiality keeps it fresh for 16 pages - less than a ten-minute read - Fly's guileless, wry commentary on how stupid the situation he's in is doesn't work for all for 60,000 words. My reservations about Fly as a protagonist are dwarfed by my problems with boring it is to hear somebody *describe* a videogame they played once - while it was still in beta.
